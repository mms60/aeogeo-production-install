How Do You Get Your Website on Google Search?

By AEOGEO Search

Quick Answer

Getting a website onto Google Search takes three steps: create a Google Search Console property for your site, verify that you own it, and submit a sitemap so Google can find your pages. Google decides independently whether to crawl and index each page. Search Console gets you discovered and lets you monitor what happens next, but it does not force indexing or rankings.

Google Search Console is a free tool, but setting it up correctly matters. The steps below show you how to create the right property, complete verification, submit your sitemap, request indexing for important pages, and use the reports to find problems before they cost you visibility.

Before You Start: Check Whether Bots Can Crawl Your Site

Before you set up Search Console, confirm that Googlebot can fetch your pages. A misconfigured robots.txt file that tells crawlers to stay away can hide the whole site, and no verification or sitemap step will fix that. Use our free Robots Text Generator to test access and create a corrected file. Our robots.txt guide for AI bots explains the rules behind the check, including blocks that happen outside robots.txt.

What Is Google Search Console?

Google Search Console (GSC) is a free tool from Google that shows you how your website appears in Google Search. It is aimed at site owners, not advertisers. You do not need to buy ads to use it, and Google does not charge for any part of it.

What Search Console Shows You

GSC tells you three things that matter for visibility:

  • Whether Google knows your pages exist. The Indexing report shows which pages Google has crawled and indexed, and which ones it skipped, with the reason.
  • What people search before clicking. The Performance report shows the queries where your site appeared, your impressions, clicks, and average position.
  • Whether anything is broken. GSC reports technical problems such as crawl errors, mobile usability issues, and security or manual actions.

Where to Sign In

Google Search Console lives at https://search.google.com/search-console. Make sure you are signed in with the Google account you want to own the property long-term.

Step 1: Create a Property

When you add a website, GSC asks you to choose between two property types.

Domain Property

A Domain property covers every subdomain and both http and https versions of your site (for example, example.com, www.example.com, and blog.example.com all in one). This requires DNS verification.

URL Prefix Property

A URL prefix property covers one exact address, such as https://example.com. This allows more verification methods, including uploading a file to your site.

Which Property Type Should You Choose?

For most small businesses, a Domain property is the better choice: it captures everything under your domain in one report. If you cannot edit DNS records, a URL-prefix property with file verification works fine.

Add your property with the Add property button in GSC and enter your website address.

Step 2: Verify Ownership

Verification proves to Google that you control the site. The most common methods are below.

DNS Record Verification

For Domain properties, GSC gives you a TXT record to add at your domain registrar. DNS changes can take from minutes to a couple of days to propagate, so verification may not succeed immediately.

HTML File Upload

For URL prefix properties, download the small verification file GSC provides and upload it to your site's root folder.

HTML Tag

Paste a meta tag into the head section of your homepage.

Through Your Hosting Platform

Many hosts and website builders, including platforms that manage DNS for you, offer one-click or guided Google verification.

Do Not Remove the Verification Token

Whichever method you use, do not remove the verification token after verifying. GSC re-checks ownership periodically, and removing it can cut off your access and your data history.

Step 3: Submit Your Sitemap

If your sitemap is not ready, the XML sitemap and crawl-depth guide explains what belongs in it and how to validate it. The file should return XML at a public URL before you submit it.

Submit It in Google Search Console

Submit the sitemap in GSC under Sitemaps: type the URL of your sitemap file and click submit. From then on, GSC reports how many URLs Google discovered from it and how many were processed.

Submitting a sitemap is a request, not a command. Pages get indexed when Google's systems decide they are worth crawling. Fresh, useful, well-linked pages get picked up faster than duplicate or thin ones.

Step 4: Speed Up Indexing for Key Pages

For a handful of important pages, you can use the URL Inspection tool at the top of GSC. Paste a page URL, and the tool shows whether that exact page is indexed. If it is not, choose Request indexing to put it in Google's crawl queue.

Use Request Indexing Sparingly

Use this for individual important pages, not bulk submissions. Requesting indexing does not guarantee that Google will index the page or that it will rank.

Step 5: Monitor and Act on What You See

Once verified, give GSC a few days to accumulate data, then build a light routine around it.

Weekly

Check the Indexing report for "Page is not indexed" reasons on pages that matter, and fix what the report highlights: noindex tags, redirects, canonical conflicts, or blocked resources.

Monthly

Review Performance for queries gaining or losing clicks, so you know what content to keep improving.

Whenever You Publish or Change Something Important

Use URL Inspection to confirm the page is reachable and request indexing when appropriate.

What Google Search Console Does Not Do

Honest limits, so expectations stay grounded:

  • It does not guarantee indexing. Google indexes pages at its discretion; submission only improves discovery.
  • It does not affect rankings directly. There is no paid or expedited tier; rankings depend on relevance, competition, and site quality.
  • It does not show AI visibility. GSC covers traditional Google Search results, not Google AI Overviews' citations or AI chatbot referrals. For that, measure AI citation and referral channels separately.
  • Data is delayed. Performance data typically lags by a couple of days, and aggregate reports are sampled rather than exhaustive.

Key Takeaways

  1. Check crawl access before anything else.
  2. A Domain property plus DNS verification is the cleanest setup for most businesses.
  3. Submit your sitemap once, then let monitoring take over. Resubmitting repeatedly adds nothing.
  4. URL Inspection is for individual important pages; the Indexing report is for the site as a whole.
  5. GSC is the foundation of search visibility, but AI-driven discovery through AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and other assistants is a separate channel with separate signals.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need Google Search Console to rank in Google?

No. Search Console is free and shows how Google sees your site, but it does not guarantee indexing or affect rankings directly, and there is no paid or expedited tier.

Should I use a Domain property or a URL prefix property?

For most small businesses a Domain property is the better choice because it covers every subdomain and both the http and https versions of your site in one report. If you cannot edit DNS records, a URL prefix property with file verification works fine.

How long does Domain property verification take?

It depends on DNS propagation. Changes can take from minutes to a couple of days, so verification may not succeed immediately after you add the TXT record.

Does Request indexing guarantee my page will appear in Google?

No. Request indexing puts a page in Google's crawl queue, but it does not guarantee that Google will index the page or that it will rank. Use it for individual important pages rather than bulk submissions.

Can Google Search Console show AI Overview citations?

No. Search Console covers traditional Google Search results, not Google AI surfaces, so it cannot report AI Overview citations. Use it for indexing, query and click data, and measure AI visibility with a separate prompt-based process.
